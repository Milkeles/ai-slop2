# Part III — Return

They met at the bottom of Quarry Lane at midnight. Tavi was already there under the streetlight, hands in his pockets.

"I gave it to him," he said before anyone could speak. "It looked like you. It had your coat. I gave it to him because I wanted my three days and I didn't want to think."

Nobody shouted. Ines had been ready to shout for twenty minutes, and when she saw his face she found she didn't want to.

"He'll be on the gantry," she said. "Where it came up."

She led, and she led by foresight, ten seconds at a time. Left at the bins. Wait, a car. Now. Over the wall, not there, there. Her ears burned. Dobri jogged behind with a tissue up one nostril. Under the slap of their feet he could hear something else, a high thin whine like a wet finger on a glass. Gran's thread, getting thinner.

"How long?" Ines asked.

"Not long."

The Bowl was black, and the water at the bottom held the stars. The gantry reached out over it into the dark, and at its far end something glowed.

Pell stood there with the Coil set on a crossbeam in front of him and a sledgehammer in his hands. Soft white lights drifted around him like jellyfish.

"Go home," he called. He sounded very tired. "I'm doing this for you."

Tavi was already unbuckling his brace.

"Wait," Ines said. "I'll call it."

He stepped out. Ines stood on the rim with her watch up in front of her face and looked ahead.

The walkway was rotten, so he took the beam beside it. A little way along, where the metal had rusted through years ago, a new plank lay across the gap, clean grey steel, perfectly solid. Ines saw it too. Then, seven seconds ahead, she saw Tavi put his weight on it, and his foot went straight through, and he dropped into the black.

"Stop! Not that one. The rusty one, on the left."

He froze with one foot in the air. Then he set it down on the ugly orange beam that looked ready to crumble, and it held.

"Two more, then jump the gap. Now. Good. Duck."

Lights swung at his head and he ducked through them. They had no weight and made no sound, but they were bright, and he was crossing the thing that had broken him in April, in the dark. Ines kept talking. Her nose was bleeding into her mouth and she kept talking.

Pell raised the hammer.

Tavi was too far away. Ines could see that he was too far away.

Dobri shut his eyes and went down into the echoes. Past this week. Past Tavi's yell in April. Further, through years, through the shouts of quarrymen and the grinding of the conveyor, the burn in his ears turning to real pain, metal filling his mouth. Down to another dry summer, this same gantry, and a woman's voice calling across the water.

He opened his mouth. He didn't know if it would come out. It came out.

"Gussie!" The voice wasn't his. It was warm and hoarse. "Gussie, come home. I don't need your face to know you."

Pell stopped with the hammer over his head.

He looked across the dark at the small boy on the rim, bleeding and swaying, with a dead woman's words in his mouth. The hammer came down slowly until its head rested on the beam. He was crying without making any sound.

Tavi lifted the Coil out from under his hands.

They sat on the gantry in a ring, all four of them.

"Your seeing won't work in there," Pell told Ines. "Time doesn't go forward in the Fold."

"So I go in blind."

"Yes."

She put her hand on the Coil beside the others and closed her eyes.

She looked ahead and saw nothing. Not darkness. Nothing at all, like a page torn out of a book.

The Fold was dark this time. The ridges were grey, the valleys nearly black, the threads as faint as cobweb. Gran's thread ran from Dobri to the spiral, so thin it was hardly there.

"I can't see where any of it goes," Ines said.

Pell stood beside them, older here and more stooped. Amber ridges glowed all around him, and not one of them had a thread.

"Then I'll give you light," he said.

He didn't use them. He used them up. One after another his ridges flared, and light rolled out of him across the whole Fold, gold, every valley lit, every thread shining clear to the horizon. As each ridge went out he looked a little more puzzled, and Ines understood that he was losing her, the woman with the scarf, piece by piece. He didn't stop.

"Go," he said.

Tavi went first. Pip's threads were the shortest, and he ran them the way he ran everything. At the end of each one he could see a small sleeping girl with one sock off. He let go, and felt his balance drain out of him like water from a bath.

Ines walked hers. She didn't know what was at the end, or what she would forget. She found her mum asleep on the sofa in her scrubs, and she let go without counting.

Dobri ran.

Gran's thread was fraying as he went, strands peeling away into the gold. The whine was in his teeth. Everything he had heard that summer ran along it with him, the echoes and the arguments, the quarry and the boy, and he knew he was carrying it home and wouldn't keep any of it. He reached the end. The thread was down to a single strand.

He let go.

When they opened their eyes it was getting light.

They were sitting on a rusty gantry over a quarry, cold and stiff, with dried blood on their faces. Ines looked at her watch and then at the drop. She had no idea why she was here, and that frightened her more than the drop.

Tavi found a black knee brace lying on the beam beside him. He looked at it for a while. Then he buckled it on.

Dobri looked at the old man at the end of the gantry. "Sorry," he said politely. "Do I know you?"

Pell smiled. His hand lay open and empty in his lap, and no light came from it. He looked out over the water like a man with nowhere he had to be.

The fossil sat on the crossbeam between them, grey and cold, an ordinary stone.

## Epilogue — Quince

The Coil sits in a locked glass case in the Weighhouse now, between a trilobite and a slab of fossil fern. The label is handwritten in brown ink: *Ammonite. Found twice. Do not touch.*

Hana went back to work in September. On her first day off she put the lunch money under the fruit bowl, then stood in Ines's doorway holding a list.

"You said you wanted to start running with Tavi in the mornings," she said. "You wrote it on the fridge. And you wanted me to teach you Grandma's soup."

Ines had no memory of writing any of it. She said yes to both.

Pip told Tavi at breakfast that she'd dreamed she was running along the gantry at the quarry, really fast, with no hands. Tavi said it sounded dangerous and told her to eat her cereal.

In Gran's kitchen the jam was on. The windows were steamed up and the whole flat smelled of quince.

Gran was sharp again, sharper than she had been in years. She called Dobri by his name every time, sometimes twice in a sentence, just to enjoy it. And she knew things. A fossil on a ledge. A boy running along a beam. An old man in a canvas coat. She knew them the way you know a story someone has told you, though nobody had.

She set the silver cassette recorder on the table, put in a new tape and pushed it across to him.

"You won't remember it," she said, "so I'll tell it. You keep the tape."

Dobri put on the headphones. The red light came on.

"It was the driest summer since 1979," Gran said, "and the water in Cutter's Bowl was very low."
