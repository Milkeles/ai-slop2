# Part II — Echoes

Ten days later, Ines could see ten seconds ahead.

She timed it every morning with her watch. A wasp landing on the gantry rail. Tavi opening his mouth to say something stupid. She saw each one, then watched it happen.

Tavi didn't need ten seconds. He had the gantry. In April he had fallen from it and torn his knee. Now he ran along the beam beside the walkway, which was as wide as his foot.

"Look, no brace!" he shouted from the far end.

"Look, no brain," Ines shouted back.

Dobri sat on the warm rock with his headphones on and the recorder running. He could hear a week back now. Going further cost more. His ears burned and his nose bled, every day now. He kept tissues in his cardigan pocket.

And there were gaps. On Thursday Ines came back to herself at seven in the evening, sunburnt, with no idea how she had got there. The last thing she remembered was lunch.

"Five hours," she said. "I've lost five hours."

"You were here," Dobri said. "You were fine. You beat Tavi at cards."

"I don't remember beating Tavi at cards. That's the worst part."

She wrote a rule on the back of her hand in blue biro and held it up. *ONE TOUCH A DAY.*

"Fine," Tavi said.

He touched the Coil twice that day. She saw him do it ten seconds before he did it, and she let it happen, because ten seconds wasn't long enough to think of what to say.

At home, things kept going wrong, and nobody joined them up.

Hana came home at two in the morning, four hours early, and sat at the kitchen table in her coat. Ines found her there.

"I nearly gave a man the wrong dose," Hana said. "Ten times the right amount. Marta caught it." She turned her hands over on the table as if they belonged to someone else. "I've given that drug a thousand times. They sent me home. They said I'm tired."

"You are tired," Ines said. She made tea. She checked the gas twice.

On Saturday Pip went to the town pool. She had been swimming since she was four, and her towel had a badge for fifty lengths sewn on it. Halfway across the deep end she rolled sideways, like a toy boat with water inside it, and went under. A lifeguard pulled her out. She said the water kept tipping.

Tavi heard about it at dinner. He went to his room and lay on his bed and didn't run anywhere.

And Gran, in the flat above the bakery, stopped calling Dobri by his name.

"Will the boy want soup?" she asked the kitchen.

"I'm right here, Gran."

She looked at him kindly and carefully, the way you look at a neighbour's child. "Yes. Of course you are."

That night Dobri took the Coil out from under his bed and carried it into Gran's room. She was asleep, small under the blanket, her lilac cardigan folded over the chair. The room smelled of quince jam and old paper.

He only wanted to hear her. She used to be the town's memory. She had recorded the old people of Limeburn on a hundred tapes. If he went deep enough into the echoes, he might hear her telling their stories again. He put his palm on the spiral and closed his eyes.

He didn't hear anything. He fell.

He was standing in a landscape of pale ridges, pink and white, rolling away like folded dunes. Between them ran narrow valleys that glowed a soft blue. The sky was violet and empty, with no sun and no stars. Far off, in the middle of everything, a huge grey stone spiral sat half sunk in the ground.

Some of the ridges near him glowed amber, warm, like lamps behind curtains. He counted them. Eleven. Twelve.

From each amber ridge a thread of light ran away across the land, through the great spiral, and on toward the dark horizon. He touched one. It hummed, and the smell came up off it so strongly his eyes watered.

Quince.

He followed the thread out of the Fold, across the dark bedroom, to the small shape in the bed. Every one of his amber folds was tied to Gran. Along the threads, slowly, things were sliding toward him, and he could hear them going. A wedding in 1962. A dog called Bisa. The name of the street where she was born. His own name, in her voice, moving out of her and into him.

He grabbed the nearest thread and walked it, hand over hand, all the way to her. At the end there was nothing to do but let go. So he did.

He opened his eyes on the floor beside her bed, his nose bleeding onto his cardigan. Gran stirred. "Bisa," she murmured, smiling. "Good dog."

Dobri tried to remember how he'd got home from the Bowl that afternoon. That part was gone.

"It isn't giving me anything," he whispered. "It's taking her."

[[img:threads]]

He rang Ines at one in the morning. Ines rang Tavi. Nobody slept.

Ines lay in the dark and thought about the hand gel. Every time she had touched the Coil she had smelled her mum. Every time, her mum had got a little worse at deciding things.

Tavi was quiet on the phone for a long time. Then he said, "Bubblegum," in a very small voice, and hung up.

In the morning they went to the Weighhouse.

Pell let them in without a word. He sat down by the projector and studied his chalky hands.

"Nineteen seventy-nine," he said. "Same drought. I was thirteen. I found it on my own and woke it on my own, so all of mine came from one person." He switched the projector on. "My mother."

The square of light on the wall wavered and became a face. A woman in her forties, with a wide mouth, tired eyes and a scarf over her hair. She was only light, and so real that Tavi stepped back.

"This is her," Pell said. "It's all I kept."

He told them the rest in a flat voice, like a man reading a label. Every fold he took made him better with light. Every fold made his mother worse with faces. Strangers first, then neighbours, then him. By the end of that summer he knew why. And he waited, because the light was beautiful, and because he was thirteen and thought there would be time.

"There wasn't. The threads thin. When they snap, the fold is yours forever, and they never get it back. All you can do then is burn it. Not use it. Use it up, all at once, and whatever you kept in it goes too." The woman on the wall smiled at nobody. "She died in 1983. She called me 'the nice young man'. I threw the stone into the deepest part of the Bowl and hoped it would stay there."

Ines was staring at the Coil in Dobri's arms. "In the Fold. All the threads went through the spiral?"

"All of them," Dobri said.

"So the spiral holds them. If the Coil breaks..."

"Every thread snaps at once," Pell said. "And nobody ever gets hurt by it again."

"Except them," Dobri said. "Gran, and Pip, and your mum. They'd never get anything back."

"Then we give it back," Tavi said. "Dobri already did one."

Pell's face shut like a door. "You can't. I tried."

Dobri said nothing. Under the ticking of the clock he could hear the boy again, in this room, years ago, saying *tomorrow, I'll do it tomorrow.* That wasn't trying. That was waiting. But he kept quiet, because he was the one who listened, not the one who spoke.

They argued all the way back to the Bowl. Ines wanted to go into the Fold that night. Dobri wanted to go now.

"Three more days," Tavi said. "The trials are on Saturday. Three days, and then I give it all back. I swear."

"Pip nearly drowned," Ines said.

"I know Pip nearly drowned!" "I'm the one who caught her. I'm asking for three days."

He took the Coil home in his rucksack. Nobody could have outrun him.

At eleven that night something tapped on his bedroom window. Ines was standing on the flat garage roof, close to the window, in her mustard windbreaker, with a finger to her lips. She held up a sheet of paper. On it, in her blocky capitals: *LET ME HIDE IT. HE KNOWS WHERE YOU LIVE.*

He opened the window. She didn't speak. She only held out her hands. That was strange for Ines. He gave her the rucksack anyway, and her arms dipped under its weight, a little lower than Ines's would have. She turned and climbed down off the roof, slow and stiff, like someone much older.

His phone buzzed on the bed.

*are you awake? can't sleep. are you ok* — Ines.

He rang Dobri.

Dobri came running in his slippers. He stood under Tavi's window, shut his eyes and listened. Through the hot ache in his ears he caught the last echo left in the street, a low, rough voice muttering to itself.

"Where it came up, it gets broken."
